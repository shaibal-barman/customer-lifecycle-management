# 5. Value Evidence and Retention

Value is easier to trust when the evidence follows a clear chain:

> **Customer action → operational change → business consequence → next decision**

A feature used is not automatically value delivered. The customer must be able to see what changed in their work and why it matters.

## Choose evidence that the customer recognizes

Useful measures depend on the customer's objective. Examples include:

- time required to complete an important workflow;
- completion or exception rate;
- reliability of a service commitment;
- resolution time for recurring issues;
- adoption by the roles that matter;
- visibility available to customer leadership; and
- progress against an agreed milestone.

Avoid reporting a large set of metrics that does not support a decision. A smaller group of explainable measures is more useful than a dashboard with no clear implication.

## Value review structure

1. **Objective:** What outcome did the customer identify as important?
2. **Progress:** What changed since the last review?
3. **Evidence:** Which measure or example supports that conclusion?
4. **Friction:** What still limits the outcome?
5. **Decision:** What needs to be agreed now?
6. **Ownership:** Who will do what, and when will progress be reviewed?

Use the [Value Review Template](../templates/value-review-template.md) to prepare this conversation.

## Separate activity from impact

| Activity statement | Value statement |
| --- | --- |
| “Thirty users attended training.” | “The two roles responsible for the workflow can now complete it without manual intervention.” |
| “Usage increased.” | “More work is completed through the intended process, reducing an avoidable handoff.” |
| “The issue was fixed.” | “The recurring exception has not appeared during the agreed validation period.” |

The value statement explains why the activity matters.

## Retention is built before the renewal conversation

Renewal risk grows when value evidence is collected late, stakeholder expectations drift, or open commitments lose ownership. A reliable retention rhythm therefore includes:

- early agreement on the customer's definition of success;
- visible progress and open risks;
- consistent follow-through;
- evidence that the product or service supports an important outcome; and
- a next value milestone before the current one becomes routine.

The renewal conversation should summarize an established value story, not create one at the last moment.
