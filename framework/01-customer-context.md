# 1. Start with the Customer's Operating Reality

A customer request is not always the whole problem. The first description may point to a feature, a report, a service issue, or a training gap. Before choosing an action, I try to understand the operating consequence behind it.

## The context brief

The brief should be short enough to review before a customer conversation and specific enough to guide a decision.

| Field | What to capture |
| --- | --- |
| Desired outcome | What the customer needs to achieve in their operation—not only what they want from the product |
| Current workflow | How the work happens today, including people, systems, handoffs, and exceptions |
| Operational pressure | The deadline, service commitment, cost, volume, or customer promise affected |
| Stakeholders | Who uses the solution, who experiences the outcome, and who makes the decision |
| Friction | Where work slows, fails, repeats, or loses ownership |
| Existing workaround | What the customer or internal team is already doing to cope |
| Success evidence | What observable change would show that the situation improved |
| Constraints | Access, policy, timing, product limitation, data quality, or team capacity |

## Questions that expose the real issue

- What happens in the customer's operation before this problem appears?
- Who notices it first, and who carries the consequence?
- Is this new, recurring, or becoming more frequent?
- What has the customer already tried?
- If nothing changes, what commitment becomes difficult to keep?
- What would the customer accept as evidence of progress?

## A useful problem statement

Use one sentence:

> **[Customer role] needs to complete [workflow] so that [business outcome] remains reliable. The main constraint is [verified friction], and progress will be visible when [evidence].**

This format prevents a feature request from being treated as the problem before the underlying workflow is understood.

## Example: the first request was not the full problem

A customer says, “We need a better report.” A quick response would be to discuss report fields. Discovery may show that the real issue is different: teams across several locations use inconsistent escalation labels, so leadership cannot compare unresolved work accurately.

The response now changes. The immediate need may be a shared escalation definition and ownership rule, followed by a reporting change only if the data becomes reliable. The customer asked for a report; the operating problem was inconsistent workflow data.

## Exit criteria

Context is sufficient when the team can state:

1. the customer outcome;
2. the consequence of the current gap;
3. the strongest available evidence;
4. the next decision required; and
5. who owns that decision.

If any of these is missing, the next action is discovery—not solutioning.
